Protect Girls Sports

Common sense is not merely a slogan; it is the faculty by which we perceive the plain truths of our existence. In the realm of athletics, a plain truth is currently under siege...

By Mark Greenstein | Opinion

Common sense is not merely a slogan; it is the faculty by which we perceive the plain truths of our existence. In the realm of athletics, a plain truth is currently under siege: that biological differences between the sexes matter, and that fairness requires we acknowledge them.

It has been over fifty years since Title IX was enacted. Its purpose was explicit and noble—to ensure that young women had the same opportunities as young men to compete, to strive, and to achieve glory on the playing field. It was a recognition that without distinct categories, female athletes would be effectively erased from the podium. Today, we face a peculiar regress where the very category of 'girls' sports' is being dismantled in the name of a new, abstract definition of inclusion.

Protect Girls Sports (Continued from Page 1)

We are told that to exclude biological males who identify as females from girls' sports is a form of bigotry. But let us apply the lamp of experience. Sports are discriminative by nature. We discriminate by age—we do not force 12-year-olds to wrestle 18-year-olds. We discriminate by weight in boxing and wrestling—we do not pit the featherweight against the heavyweight. We do this not out of malice, but out of a fierce commitment to fairness. Sex is the most fundamental of these biological categories.

When a biological male competes in female athletics, the competitive balance is shattered. The bone density, muscle mass, and lung capacity conferred by male puberty provide an insurmountable advantage that no amount of training or 'identification' can erase. To ignore this is to tell every girl on the starting line that her effort, her discipline, and her dreams are secondary to the validation of another's personal identity.

The Town Hall: Democracy's Purest Form

By Sarah Miller | Civics

In hundreds of small towns across Vermont and New Hampshire, the first Tuesday in March isn't just another day; it's Town Meeting Day. This tradition represents the purest form of direct democracy remaining in the United States. It's a place where a single voice can change the course of a town's budget, and where disagreement is tempered by the fact that you will see your opponent at the grocery store tomorrow.

In an era of increasing polarization and digital siloization, the physical proximity of the town hall forces a level of civility that is often lost online. Here, civics is not an abstract concept; it is a lived experience of collective responsibility.

Tariffs: Hurtful, especially in the northeast

By Mark Stewart Greenstein | Economy

The few who support tariffs need to start again. I say “few” because almost nobody likes the consequences of tariffs, direct or indirect. Many who support President Trump align to approve of tariffs simply because Trump is behind it and they are convinced he’s playing 4D chess. Separate them, and there are few Americans who approve.

The direct consequence of tariffs are: consumers pay more. Or at least “more than necessary”. If an importer really can absorb the cost of the tariff and keep a decent margin, then without the tariff, that importer COULD be offering consumers a still-lower price.

And where is the excess we lay out going? Not to the importer. Not to the American retailer. It’s to the U.S. government, a very UN-worthy receiver. Our government already taxes us too much; it does not deserve a windfall from our purchases of imported goods.

The indirect consequence is even worse: foreign nations enact reciprocal tariffs on U.S. exports. That has not happened in a large way yet; but all it takes is a populist leader abroad to say “I’m standing up to Trump”. His consumers will then have THREE reasons to not buy from U.S. firms: • Higher prices to them, • Akin to war, they adopt “Don’t Buy American”, • New manufacturers form in those nations to compete against us.

Any one of these three is devastating to American exporters. Indirectly it hurts us all, because less money in the pockets of the employees and owners means less they can spend domestically. And if it’s dramatically less revenue, employees get laid off. That’s ZERO income for them and greatly reduced spending to businesses around them.

What does it take to trigger this retaliation? For some foreign leaders it would take a lot, but if there’s anything that can supply instant international animus, it’s the not-so careful words of a bombastic U.S. President.

If the Trump Administration REALLY Wants Tariffs, Use Congress. Congress, should a majority in each House approve, has the sole responsibility to impose tariffs. This comes straight from the U.S. Constitution. The Framers did not want states competing with one another to have lower tariffs, so they added the language in Article I sec 8, that “all Duties, Imposts and Excises shall be uniform throughout the United States”. From the outset, the Trump tariffs were an end-around.

Why has Trump eschewed Congress thus far? Because no Democrats and very few Republicans will vote for raised tariffs.**

The RIGHT policy The world has known since 1776 that the wealth of nations (ours and theirs) increases with free trade. Mercantilism is jingoistic and can lead to war. Even when it doesn’t lead to war, it punishes consumers. A few leaders look tough on video while the rest of us suffer.

I am a candidate for Congress, a liberty-minded Independent. That’s neither left nor right (we call liberty-mindedness “above”). If elected, citizens can count on my vote to be a steadfast “no” on tariffs. They are unwise. Even if the revenue dramatically brought down the debt (it doesn’t; $200 B wind-drift barely makes a dent in a $38,000 B mountain of debt) tariffs are bad. Tariffs act as a tax on us.

I will never vote to use a tariff as a weapon to prod a foreign nation’s policy change. We have enough clout and enough financial tools, from freezing foreign assets to outright urges to boycott sales, to propel change. Unless a nation truly deserves a financial sanction, let American businesses and American consumers trade with their businesses, and don’t put upon us a tax every time we buy Italian shoes, Moroccan shirts, Chinese toys, Colombian coffee, or New Zealand fruit.

With six Supreme Court Justices voting against him, our President now looks to administrative loopholes and legal interpretations to do what’s not right in the first place. He should let it go. I’m Donald Trump’s ally on many things. He’s shown by effective use of the military that he can move nations toward compliance; he does not need a punitive tariff regime to do so.

** Only 11 states have a positive trade balance; nine are politically “red”, led by oil exporter Louisiana. None is in the northeast; even the Congressional Republicans from the other 39 states would likely vote against raised tariffs. Indeed, one of the worst trade-deficit states, just behind the two biggest “losers”, New Jersey and Tennessee, is MICHIGAN. Yes, producers of the most expensive items consumers can buy, Michigan’s trucks and cars are virtually off-limits to international buyers, so high are THEIR protective tariffs against U.S. car manufacturers.

Health Insurance Spike and Poor Government Decisions

When it comes to health insurance, the Affordable Care Act (ACA), the lack of tort reform, our inflating American dollar, fraud...

By Sen. James Libby | Opinion

When it comes to health insurance, the Affordable Care Act (ACA), the lack of tort reform, our inflating American dollar, fraud, and several other key issues have put Maine in an incredibly vulnerable position. A solution to the health insurance problem will be elusive; but American political leaders must face the challenge now – not later.

A presentation made to the Maine Legislature’s Health Coverage, Insurance and Financial Services Committee in November tipped off lawmakers to what is coming. For the 64,678 Mainers who get their insurance from Maine’s CoverME, the state-based health care exchange operated under the ACA, health care premiums are set to increase by an average of 77% in 2026 for households receiving the federal enhanced premium tax credit (EPTC).

For many Maine families, it means a much larger percentage of their income will go toward health insurance premiums – that is, if they even decide to continue coverage. Those in the highest income bracket who currently receive the EPTC will lose the subsidy completely. And according to the office that oversees the Maine Health Insurance Marketplace, that could add up to tens of thousands of dollars per year.

One problem is the ACA, known to most as Obamacare, has been failing us for years. Higher subsidization of it through the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 and then extended by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 only masked a problem that had been growing since ACA was first implemented in 2014. The nearly 600,000 Mainers with health insurance through an employer (including me) have already experienced the rollercoaster of double-digit rate increases over the past decade.

In fact, the average annual cost of insurance for single employees covered under an employer-sponsored plan was $5,571 in 2013, the year before ACA took effect. Today, that same coverage is $9,325, according to the 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey released by KFF this year. That’s a staggering 67% increase under ACA, certainly not what we were promised by then-President Barack Obama. Unfortunately, health insurance premium increases are about to rise still higher.

The ACA was billed as health care reform; but it did very little besides enriching insurance companies by forcing people to purchase policies under penalty of an additional tax. Health care providers on the other side of the insurance equation can charge whatever they want because the government has subsidized their patients’ insurance premiums. It demonstrates the ACA never solved the true problem with health care – it simply became one of three major cost drivers of it.

ACA has cost taxpayers dearly and has been ripe for fraud and abuse. The Government Accountability Office reported in December 2025 that millions of improper enrollments accounted for $27 billion in taxpayer losses annually. It’s simply more proof of a broken system that only drives up costs in the long run.

Another problem is the American dollar itself. Recently the Federal Reserve System's M1 money supply expansion topped $19 trillion dollars, up from about $4 trillion in February 2020. The money supply was increased to accommodate interest rate cuts; and we have just experienced two rate cuts in the past two Fed meetings. The reality of this is that if all other intervening variables were held constant since 2019, health care costs will eventually more than quadruple from that 2020 date, since health care services are needs – not wants – and good healthcare is hard to find. Unfortunately, no variables held constant and upward price pressures are at severe levels.

If I were to advise the President on one thing, it would be to stop his well-intentioned advocacy for interest rate reduction. Rate reduction often works well by providing opportunities for consumer and industry borrowing and refinancing. But in the current inflated environment, that strategy will help some and hurt many others. It will cause another spiral of inflation in areas of the economy where more supply of goods and services are harder to foster, which is the case with healthcare.

Another cost driver is that the ACA and other insurance eras failed to reform tort laws that insurance companies charge medical providers enormous premiums to cover. The lawsuit lottery condition of the United States has led to higher premiums and defensive medicine. At some point, we will need to face this problem – we have been ignoring it for decades.

The ACA also didn’t address our unhealthy, aging population, force price transparency or eliminate the relic known as "certificate of need (CON)," through which states like Maine limit competition. Maine reformers will tell you that CON has been reformed but much of it is still in law. Consolidation in the industry reduces competition and has also driven up prices in sheltered markets like Maine's.

The impending loss of the EPTC is a problem for Maine families; but it’s one that Democrats created by increasing the subsidies to begin with. It only hid the problem and delayed the inevitable. What’s happening now is the CoverME premiums are finally catching up to what the rest of us have already endured since ACA was implemented over a decade ago.

The entire health insurance system is collapsing under its own weight. The solution is to reform the health care system itself by addressing each of the problems I outlined above. I was involved in doing just that when worker's compensation insurers left Maine in the early 1990s. That fix, led by Governor McKernan, was outstanding. Unfortunately, its complexities pale in comparison to the health insurance premium problem we face today.

Social Security - Rep. Larson defends the indefensible

Oh my. Congressman John Larson has made “saving Social Security” his banner issue. Only a few highly protective or deeply out-of-touch people think this is a system worth saving...

By Mark Stewart | Liberty & Politics

Oh my. Congressman John Larson has made “saving Social Security” his banner issue. Only a few highly protective or deeply out-of-touch people think this is a system worth saving. It should have been ended long ago.

By 1999 it was clear to anyone that Social Security could not pay its promised returns without a large tax increase. By 2005, Social Security was, by any business definition, bankrupt. Twenty more years of deficits, higher life-expectancies, and a still lower worker-to-payee ratio (now 3:1; when the program was inaugurated it was 18:1), Social Security cannot right itself absent a dramatic cut in benefits, or a tripling of the tax it foists on workers.

The 1936 system of SS funds being set aside and building interest to be paid decades later lasted all of three years. By 1939, politicians had raided the Social Security Trust. Since then, some or (now) all of its funds go right to the U.S. Treasury, for Congress to spend as it wishes.

Social Security will always be a fat trove of money for politicians to take. That is because: 1. Few people notice the mis-directed spending, 2. Those who notice can’t do anything to stop the politicians or the “Trustees”, and 3. Those who are hurt most have no political clout: the very young and the unborn.

The harm to young workers is significant. Social Security (including Medicare), extracts 15.3% of a mid-income worker’s salary. One who “earns” $70,000 a year thus sees only $59,300. Ask one hundred workers whose take-home is $59,300 minus income taxes whether they’d prefer an added $10,700 and forgo a mystical Social Security benefit 20, 30, 40, or 50 years away. At least 90% will say “yes”.

For those few who would like to entrust their money to the government for those many years (and hope to not die before collecting) there’s a beautiful option that Larson doesn’t countenance: make Social Security optional. The people wanting the government option can still have it; a slimmed-down Social Security system will still give the same percentage returns; it does not require 100,000,000 participants. It doesn’t even require 1,000,000 (see long-standing private wealth managers who do very well for a mere 1000 or so clients.).

Larson is not alone. His blind spot appears to infect every elected Democrat. Not one Democrat has announced a vision other than government-controlled tethering of our earnings. Not one Democrat decries the oppressive unfairness that Social Security foists on younger workers. And ironically, not one elected Democrat even points out that Social Security is the most regressive tax in America. High income earners pay in at a LOWER rate than middle income and low income earners. Currently, the pay-in applies only up to $184,500**. Thus, the person earning above $184,500 annually has a smaller percentage taken out. For the $300,000 earner, the benefit is a boon: 15.3% of $184,500 is just 9.4% of $300,000; for the $750,000 earner, the benefit is laughable: 15.3% of $184,500 is a mere 3.76% of the $750,000.

Elected Democrats endorse Social Security because they like government control. The forced pay-in, before even receiving one’s paycheck, is the ultimate way to fund statism. FDR started this morass, and 90 years later, it is a fixture for elected Democrats (and some Republicans).

Why the Larson solution does not work Rep Larson’s main cog in attempted reform is to push for high income earners to pay in at the same rate. If enacted, this would be a very temporary fix. Larson’s solution neglects the pay-OUT aspect of Social Security. At some point, the high earners retire; then their benefits come due and they are HIGHER than the mid-income earner. Larson’s plan might bring a quick infusion of money, but unless the accounting is even more monstrous, the ledger must show a very high expected pay-out for every high paying-in “contributor”.

Indeed, the pay-OUT time almost certainly comes sooner than average if high income earners are fully contributing. At least four reasons gird this: 1. The highest income earners are on average older and thus closer to withdrawal age. 2. Because they don’t need the money, they are more likely to take their money out at age 59.5 than the lower income earner who calculates a bigger benefit by delaying withdrawals. 3. They are healthier, and far less likely to succumb to disease in their early 60s than lower income workers. 4. They are almost always married, so the Trust Fund will not be keeping their funds, as it does with unmarried workers who die young.

Now, if Larson, in traditional legislative fashion, is just “kicking the can down the road”, with not true fix in sight, that’s even more reason to keep his plans far away from a Congressional vote. If his plan is to fix by taxing workers at a higher rate, that’s from the classic ”tax-because-you-can” playbook.

Either version means Social Security’s purpose is defeated. Social Security was meant to be a backstop, to keep those who had saved inadequately from working until they dropped dead. Now, any attempt to “save” it means a delay of the inevitable collapse, and unfair confiscations along the way. OSDI and Medicare should be optional; if a payout is needed for current retirees and those close to retirement, then that should come from the Treasury, not from workers whose confiscated earnings will never be fairly repaid.

** Social Security was capped for two main reasons: 1) high earners rarely need the benefits in old age, and 2) high earners are influential in policy-making; few legislators felt their well-heeled constituents (including political donors) would brook a 15.3% tax on themselves.
